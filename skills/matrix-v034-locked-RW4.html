WHY IS THE SKILLS MATRIX SO BROAD?

Fair question. Shane’s always busy.

For most of his career, Shane has been paid on results — his own sales, his team’s performance, profitability, or the commercial result of a project.

That has shaped the way he looks at work. Once Shane understands how he is being measured and what the team is trying to achieve, his natural question is: how can we get there — and is there an opportunity to get there faster, more efficiently or with a better result?

Sometimes those opportunities sit comfortably within his normal responsibilities. Sometimes they sit outside the traditional scope of his role.

When they do, Shane doesn’t assume they belong to him or that he knows better than the people responsible for them. He works with the people involved, gets the appropriate agreement or approval, and looks at whether he can help facilitate a faster, cheaper or better solution that contributes to the result he is responsible for.

Sometimes that has meant learning something unfamiliar, helping another part of the business, physically doing some of the work himself, or using his own time so that the additional activity doesn’t take him away from his normal responsibilities.

The motivation is the result. If an opportunity can increase sales, improve team performance, reduce cost, create capacity or increase the velocity at which the team reaches its target, then capturing that opportunity can directly improve the result Shane is measured — and often remunerated — on.
Shane isn’t looking for other people’s jobs to do. He’s looking for opportunities that help the team get a better result.
Agree the result→Work with the team→ Work out what’s needed→Learn · Help · Build · Buy · Outsource→ Get it done→Measure the result

After 30+ years of working that way, you end up learning how to do a surprising number of things. That is why Shane’s Skills Matrix is considerably broader than his job titles.

WHERE IT STARTED

Warehouse — the beginning of the philosophy

Shane’s first job was supposed to be straightforward warehouse work: unload trucks, keep the warehouse tidy, prepare stock for collection or delivery and sometimes go out on deliveries to make sure the job was completed.

Very early on, he realised that simply completing the tasks he had been given was not necessarily the same as producing the best result for the business. He used discarded material to build shelving, created proper places for stock that had been sitting around, helped get obsolete or dead stock back toward the shop so it could be sold, and made the warehouse faster and easier to operate.

As the warehouse became more efficient, Shane had more time to help in the shop and sell. Within weeks, the commission from his part-time selling was becoming comparable to what he was being paid for the warehouse work itself.

The early lesson: Doing the task you were given and achieving the best result for the business are not always exactly the same thing. Improve the operation → create capacity → use that capacity productively → improve the result.
